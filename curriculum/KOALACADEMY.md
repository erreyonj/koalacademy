# Koalacademy: A Music Production Curriculum Using Koala Sampler

Koalacademy is a comprehensive music production curriculum designed for urban school
districts. The course is intended for students interested in learning how to produce music
using Koala Sampler, an intuitive and user-friendly music production application for iOS
devices. The curriculum guides students from beginner to advanced levels of proficiency over
the course of 20 weeks.

Throughout the course, students explore sound design, sampling, sequencing, mixing, and
effects processing. Students also learn basic music theory concepts and how to apply them in
their music production using Koala Sampler.

By the end of the course, students will have a working understanding of music production and
the skills needed to produce their own music using Koala Sampler.

### Formatting conventions

- Each unit contains two components. Each component contains five lessons.
- Lessons are identified by a component code and a number, e.g. `BMT.1`, `KSN.3`, `SRP.5`.
- Every lesson has a **Focus** statement (why the lesson exists) followed by an **Outline**
  (the teaching sequence).
- Outlines use numbered sections with nested bullets. Depth reflects teaching structure, not
  strict lecture timing.

### Pacing

Twenty weeks, five units. Each unit spans roughly four weeks: two weeks per component, five
sessions per component. Case study and work-week lessons are intentionally flexible so
instructors can expand or compress based on student progress.

## Unit structure

1. **Music Theory and Koala Sampler Navigation**
   - Component 1: Basic Music Theory — intervals, scales, chords, basic harmony, key
     signatures, time signatures, and rhythm
   - Component 2: Koala Sampler Navigation — interface and workflow, loading and
     manipulating samples, sequencing and arranging
2. **Understanding Sampling and Digital Drumming / Drum Design**
   - Component 1: Understanding Sampling — sampling as a practice, and notable works from
     producers such as Timbaland, Kanye West, and Pharrell Williams
   - Component 2: Digital Drumming / Drum Design — types of drum sounds and how they are
     used, modifying drum sounds using effects and processing
3. **Song Forms in Popular Music and Rights / Permissions Pertaining to Sampling**
   - Component 1: Song Forms in Popular Music — AABA form and simple variations, analyzing
     popular songs for their structure
   - Component 2: Sampling Rights and Permissions — ethical use of sampling, paths to get
     clearances for samples for official works
4. **Defining Your Sound and Artist Appreciation**
   - Component 1: Defining Your Sound // Applied Theory — creating a playlist of 10 songs
     that represent the student's style, and analyzing each song using tools learned in
     previous units
   - Component 2: Artist Appreciation — choosing three songs from that playlist to sample in
     a final Koala project, and presenting each song to the class
5. **Advanced Effects Processing and Performing with Koala Sampler**
   - Component 1: Advanced Effects Processing — deeper exploration of the Perform tab, and
     creative use of effects to expand on ideas
   - Component 2: Performing with Koala Sampler — live recording of arrangement patterns and
     songs using tools and practices learned in previous units

## Component 1: Basic Music Theory

### BMT.1 — Basic Music Theory

**Focus:** Establish the shared vocabulary of Western music — natural notes, major and minor
scales, intervals, and key signatures — that the rest of the course depends on.

The basics of music theory are essential for any aspiring musician or music producer.
Understanding the seven natural notes and how they form a major scale is a fundamental concept
that lays the groundwork for further study.

The seven natural notes in Western music are A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. These notes can be
arranged to form a major scale, which consists of seven notes arranged in a specific pattern of
whole steps and half steps. The pattern for a major scale is whole, whole, half, whole, whole,
whole, half. For example, the C major scale consists of C, D, E, F, G, A, and B.

Western music is often taught around the C major scale because it is the simplest and most
commonly used. The white keys on a piano represent the notes of the C major scale, and many
popular songs are written in this key. By learning the notes and intervals of the C major scale,
students can more easily understand scales and key signatures generally.

In addition to major scales, there are minor scales. While major scales tend to sound bright and
upbeat, minor scales are often associated with sad or melancholic feelings. The pattern for a
natural minor scale is whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole. For example, the A minor
scale consists of A, B, C, D, E, F, and G.

**Outline**

1. Key principles students must understand
   - Notes and their placement on the musical staff
   - Intervals and the distance between two notes
   - Scales and the patterns of whole and half steps that form them
   - Key signatures and how they represent the notes used in a particular scale or mode
   - Chords and how they are built from scales and intervals
2. Teaching approach
   - Use a piano keyboard diagram to show note placement and the whole/half step pattern of a
     scale
   - Use examples of popular songs in different keys to demonstrate the difference between
     major and minor and how each affects mood
   - Connect each concept back to production work in Koala Sampler

### BMT.3 — Relative and Parallel Keys

**Focus:** Give students a practical framework for finding samples that work together and for
moving material between keys.

Relative keys are a pair of keys that share the same key signature but start on different notes.
The major and minor scales that share a key signature are relative keys — C major and A minor
are relative keys because neither has sharps or flats.

Parallel keys are a pair of keys that share the same starting note but have different key
signatures. C major and C minor are parallel keys: same starting note, different key signature.

**Outline**

1. Relative keys
   - Definition and identification from a key signature
   - Finding the relative minor of any major key, and the reverse
2. Parallel keys
   - Definition and contrast with relative keys
   - The emotional shift between parallel major and minor
3. Producer applications
   - Quickly finding samples that work well together
   - Adjusting a sample to fit a different setting
   - Building chord progressions that use the relationships between related keys for emotional
     impact

### BMT.5 — 3/4 and 6/8 Time, and Basic Drum Sounds

**Focus:** Extend rhythmic vocabulary past 4/4 and introduce the four drum sounds students will
use to build their first beats.

**Outline**

1. 3/4 time signature
   - A simple meter with three beats per measure; the quarter note receives one beat
   - Common in waltzes and ballads
   - Practice counting and clapping along to examples of songs in 3/4
2. 6/8 time signature
   - A compound meter with six beats per measure; the eighth note receives one beat
   - Common in soul, reggae, and jazz
   - Practice counting and clapping along to examples of songs in 6/8
3. Basic drum sounds
   - **Kicks:** the most fundamental sound in a beat; provides the rhythmic foundation and is
     typically played on the first beat of each measure
   - **Snares:** provide the backbeat, typically on beats two and four; create momentum and
     drive the track forward
   - **Hi-hats:** provide rhythmic texture, typically on every eighth note or every other
     eighth note; used to create different patterns and grooves
   - **Toms:** used for fills and transitions; add variety and excitement to a beat
4. Looking ahead
   - How these four sounds combine into a first beat
   - Preview of the advanced percussion techniques covered in Unit 2

# Unit 4: Defining Your Sound and Artist Appreciation

   - Structural tendencies: how sections are built and how they change (`SONGFORM.1`–`SONGFORM.5`)

   - Students name the gap between what they make now and what they want to make
   - Writing a one-paragraph sound statement to revisit at the end of the course

   - Students note the two changes with the largest expected impact
7. Conclusion
   - Deliverable: complete arrangement plus completed documentation checklist
   - Preview of artist appreciation presentations

### AW.5 — Artist Appreciation Presentations

**Focus:** Present the three source songs to the class, honoring the original artists and
explaining the sampling plan in musical terms.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Why this presentation is called artist appreciation
   - Acknowledgment as both an ethical practice and an analytical one (`SRP.1`)
2. Presentation requirements — ten to twelve minutes per student, covering each of the three
   songs:
   - Artist, producer, year, and context of the original recording
   - A short listening excerpt with the class
   - Why the song matters to the student's sound
   - Its musical properties: key, mode, meter, groove, and form (`DYS.3`)
   - Its production properties and the specific material being sampled (`DYS.4`)
   - The sampling plan and manipulation applied in the final project
   - Credits and rights status for the source
3. Work-in-progress playback
   - Playing the current arrangement from `AW.4`
   - Pointing out where each source appears
4. Class discussion
   - Questions on analysis and sampling choices
   - Connections between different students' sources and eras
   - Recognizing shared reference points across the class
5. Assessment
   - Accuracy of the musical and production analysis
   - Depth of appreciation for the original artists
   - Clarity of the sampling rationale
   - Completeness of credits and rights documentation
6. Conclusion
   - Recap of Unit 4: a defined sound, a documented playlist, and a project underway
   - Handoff into the advanced effects and performance work of Unit 5

# Unit 5: Advanced Effects Processing and Performing with Koala Sampler

Unit folder: [units/unit-05-advanced-effects-and-performance](../units/unit-05-advanced-effects-and-performance)

Unit 5 finishes the course by pushing Koala past pattern building. Students learn to use effects
as compositional tools, place their project in the wider mobile DAW landscape, and perform their
work live for an audience.

## Component 1: Advanced Effects Processing

### AEP.1 — Perform Tab II

**Focus:** Move from knowing what each effect does to using effects expressively in combination
and in real time.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Recap of the individual effects surveyed in `KSN.3`
   - The shift from identifying effects to performing with them
2. Effect chains and order of operations
   - How effect order changes the result
   - Chains that reinforce each other, such as filter into delay
   - Chains that fight each other, and how to hear the conflict
3. Effect families and practical grouping
   - Destructive and character effects: Crush, Dirty, Ring, Comb
   - Time and space effects: Reverb, Tempo Delay, Dub
   - Rhythmic and gating effects: Stutter, Gate, Cutter
   - Pitch and modulation effects: Pitch, Talkbox, Vibroflange
   - Dynamics and control: Compressor, Filter
4. Real-time performance technique
   - Gesture control and how movement translates to musical result
   - Performing effects in time with the sequence
   - Committing a performed pass versus keeping effects live
5. Effects as arrangement tools
   - Using a filter sweep or gate change to define a new section
   - Building tension into a transition with delay and reverb
   - Creating a breakdown entirely from processing rather than new material
6. Practical exercise
   - Students take one loop from their final project and produce three distinct versions using
     effects alone
   - Class identifies which version implies which song section
7. Conclusion
   - Effects treated as composition, not decoration
   - Preview of the sequencing work in `AEP.2`

### AEP.2 — Expanding Ideas in Sequencing with FX

**Focus:** Turn short loops and unfinished ideas into full sections by combining sequencing
techniques with processing.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - The common problem of a strong loop that will not become a song
   - How sequencing and effects together solve it
2. Diagnosing a stalled idea
   - Determining whether the loop lacks contrast, movement, or direction
   - Identifying which element the listener's attention follows
3. Sequencing techniques for expansion
   - Variation through velocity and probability editing (`KSN.4`)
   - Re-sequencing existing chops into new melodic or rhythmic figures
   - Muting and reintroducing elements to imply structure
   - Changing pattern length to break a predictable cycle
4. Effects techniques for expansion
   - Processing a duplicate of an element to create a companion part
   - Using reverse, stutter, and dub to generate new material from old (`KSN.3`)
   - Resampling a processed pass as a new source (`US.5`)
5. Combining both approaches
   - Building a second section from the first without new source material
   - Creating an intro and an outro from processed fragments
   - Writing transitions that connect the sections (`SONGFORM.5`)
6. Applied work session
   - Students expand one final project loop into three connected sections
   - Instructor check-ins on contrast and pacing
7. Conclusion
   - Recap of expansion strategies
   - Preview of the individual project reviews

### AEP.3 — One-on-One Follow-Ups on Final Projects

**Focus:** Give every student individualized, rubric-based direction on their project while there
is still time to act on it.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Purpose of individual reviews
   - How the session runs while the rest of the class works independently
2. Review rubric
   - Form: does the arrangement have clear, purposeful sections (`SONGFORM.1`)
   - Groove: is the rhythm intentional, human, and consistent (`D4.2`, `D4.3`)
   - Sample use: is the source material transformed and purposeful (`US.1`–`US.5`)
   - Sonic clarity: can each element be heard in its own space (`US.4`, `D4.5`)
   - Effects: do processing choices support the arrangement (`AEP.1`, `AEP.2`)
   - Documentation: are sources, credits, and rights complete (`SRP.4`)
   - Identity: does the project reflect the sound statement from `DYS.1`
3. One-on-one conference structure
   - Student plays the project and states their own assessment first
   - Instructor responds against the rubric
   - Two required revisions and one optional stretch goal agreed and written down
4. Independent work time
   - Students not in conference continue revising
   - Peer listening pairs for quick second opinions
5. Common issues and remedies
   - Arrangements that never leave the loop
   - Overprocessing that obscures the source material
   - Drum patterns that fight the sampled groove
   - Incomplete source documentation
6. Conclusion
   - Every student leaves with a written revision list
   - Preview of the mobile DAW comparison

### AEP.4 — Koala Versus Other Mobile DAWs

**Focus:** Situate Koala within the wider iOS production landscape so students can choose tools
deliberately after the course ends.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Why tool comparison belongs in a production course
   - Transferable skills versus tool-specific skills
2. What Koala is optimized for
   - Immediate sampling and chopping
   - Speed from idea to playable pattern
   - Pad-based performance and effects
   - Where Koala's design limits a project
3. The mobile DAW landscape
   - Loop-centric and live-looping tools such as Loopy Pro
   - Traditional sequencer-style tools such as BeatMaker
   - Full multitrack environments on iOS
   - Host and plugin ecosystems, including AUv3 and Ableton Link
4. Structured comparison
   - Sampling and chopping workflow
   - Sequencing and arrangement depth
   - Mixing and effects capability
   - File management, export, and interoperability (`KSN.5`)
   - Cost and accessibility on shared or school devices
5. Choosing a tool for a task
   - When to stay in Koala for the entire project
   - When to start in Koala and finish elsewhere
   - How to move material out of Koala cleanly
   - Working across apps in a single session
6. Class exercise
   - Students recreate a short section of their project in a second app where devices allow, or
     document a migration plan where they do not
   - Discussion of what transferred easily and what did not
7. Conclusion
   - Concepts transfer even when interfaces do not
   - Preview of live set preparation

### AEP.5 — Building a Live Perform Set

**Focus:** Restructure the finished project into something performable, which is a different task
from finishing an arrangement.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - The difference between a completed arrangement and a performable set
   - What the audience needs to see and hear
2. Set design
   - Choosing which sections to perform live and which to leave fixed
   - Ordering material for energy and pacing
   - Deciding set length and where the piece ends
3. Project layout for performance
   - Grouping related chops onto adjacent pads for physical reachability (`KSN.1`)
   - Arranging patterns in the order they will be triggered (`KSN.2`)
   - Reducing the number of simultaneous decisions required
   - Labeling everything so nothing must be remembered under pressure
4. Assigning live control
   - Selecting the two or three effects that will be performed rather than programmed (`AEP.1`)
   - Choosing which elements will be muted and reintroduced live
   - Planning note repeat and fill moments (`D4.4`)
5. Rehearsal and failure planning
   - Practicing the transitions rather than the loops
   - Identifying the moments most likely to go wrong
   - Building recovery strategies for a mistrigger or a dropped section
   - Checking device readiness: storage, battery, notifications, and audio output
6. Run-throughs
   - Two timed run-throughs with peer notes
   - Revising the layout based on what actually failed
7. Conclusion
   - Deliverable: a documented set plan and performance-ready project layout
   - Handoff into the performance component

## Component 2: Performing with Koala Sampler

### PERFORMANCE.1 — Final Project

**Focus:** Lock the final project so performance work can proceed against a fixed target.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - What locking a project means and why deadlines improve work
   - Review of the revision lists from `AEP.3`
2. Final revision pass
   - Completing the two required revisions from the one-on-one conference
   - Resisting new ideas that would restart the project
   - Deciding what is finished and what is merely unfinished forever
3. Final technical pass
   - Level balance across all sections
   - Checking playback on multiple outputs, including phone speaker and headphones
   - Confirming the arrangement plays start to finish without intervention
4. Deliverables
   - Final Koala project file, saved and backed up (`KSN.5`)
   - Exported audio bounce of the full arrangement
   - Source and credits documentation for all three songs (`SRP.4`)
   - Written project statement connecting the result to the sound statement in `DYS.1`
5. Submission and archiving
   - Submitting the project file, bounce, and documentation
   - Archiving source material and stems for future use
6. Conclusion
   - Projects locked; remaining sessions focus on performance
   - Preview of the performance check-in

### PERFORMANCE.2 — Performance Check-In

**Focus:** Rehearse the set in front of a small audience and fix what breaks before the graded
performances.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Purpose of a low-stakes rehearsal
   - How the session is structured
2. Set plan review
   - Confirming each student's set plan from `AEP.5` matches their locked project
   - Verifying pad layout, labeling, and pattern order
3. Small-group run-throughs
   - Performing for three to four peers rather than the full class
   - Peer notes on clarity, pacing, and audible mistakes
   - Instructor notes on layout and control assignments
4. Diagnosing performance problems
   - Sections that are too dense to perform reliably
   - Transitions that require too many simultaneous actions
   - Effects moves that do not read to a listener
   - Silence or dead air between sections
5. Revision time
   - Simplifying layout and reassigning live control
   - Rehearsing the specific transitions that failed
6. Stage practices
   - Starting and ending cleanly
   - Managing nerves and recovering audibly from mistakes
   - Brief spoken introduction of the work
7. Conclusion
   - Revised set plans confirmed
   - Preview of live recording

### PERFORMANCE.3 — Live Recording

**Focus:** Capture a live performance pass as a recording, which demands both performance and
engineering discipline.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - The difference between a sequenced bounce and a live captured take
   - Why live takes carry energy that programmed arrangements often lack
2. Recording setup
   - Capturing performance passes within Koala (`KSN.1`, `KSN.2`)
   - Setting input and output levels to avoid clipping
   - Preparing the device: storage space, notifications off, stable power
3. Performance capture technique
   - Recording live effect moves as part of the take (`AEP.1`)
   - Recording live pattern changes and mutes
   - Playing through mistakes rather than stopping
4. Take management
   - Recording multiple complete takes
   - Labeling and organizing takes immediately (`KSN.5`)
   - Choosing a take on feel rather than technical perfection alone
5. Light post-production
   - Trimming the head and tail
   - Correcting level issues without rebuilding the performance
   - Deciding what is worth fixing and what should stay
6. Comparison and reflection
   - Comparing the live take with the sequenced bounce from `PERFORMANCE.1`
   - Discussing what each version does better
   - Selecting the version to present
7. Conclusion
   - Deliverable: one selected live recording, labeled and exported
   - Preview of class presentations

### PERFORMANCE.4 — Class Presentations I

**Focus:** Perform the final project live for the class, with the analytical context behind it.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Format and running order for the first half of the class
   - Audience expectations and etiquette
2. Presentation format — twelve to fifteen minutes per student:
   - Brief introduction of the work and the three source songs (`AW.5`)
   - Live performance of the set (`AEP.5`)
   - Walkthrough of one section, showing how the sampled material was built and processed
   - Statement of what the student learned about their own sound
3. Structured audience response
   - What the audience heard first
   - What worked musically and why
   - One specific question per listener
4. Instructor assessment
   - Musical result: form, groove, and sample use
   - Performance execution: clarity, pacing, and recovery
   - Analytical depth: does the student understand their own choices
   - Documentation and ethics: complete credits and rights record (`SRP.1`, `SRP.4`)
   - Growth relative to the sound statement in `DYS.1`
5. Reflection
   - Written self-assessment submitted after performing
   - Notes on what the student would do differently
6. Conclusion
   - First group complete
   - Preview of the second group and the course close

### PERFORMANCE.5 — Class Presentations II and Course Close

**Focus:** Complete the remaining performances and close the course by connecting the work to
what comes after it.

**Outline**

1. Introduction
   - Running order for the remaining performances
   - Same format and criteria as `PERFORMANCE.4`
2. Remaining presentations
   - Live performance, section walkthrough, and reflection per student
   - Structured audience response and instructor assessment
3. Class listening session
   - Full playback of the class's finished work as a single program
   - Identifying the range of sounds developed across the class
   - Recognizing shared influences and divergent outcomes
4. Course retrospective
   - Tracing the arc from `BMT.1` to a performed original project
   - Revisiting the `DYS.1` sound statements against the finished work
   - Naming the concepts students expect to keep using
5. Continuing beyond the course
   - Ongoing practice habits: analysis, documentation, and regular finishing
   - Releasing music: distribution, metadata, and splits (`SRP.2`)
   - Sharing and collaborating on project files, including the Blooprint Community concept
     described in [apps/blooprint-community.md](../apps/blooprint-community.md)
   - Moving into other production tools with transferable skills (`AEP.4`)
   - Pathways in production, engineering, performance, and music education
6. Conclusion
   - Final submissions confirmed: project file, bounce, live recording, documentation, and
     written reflection
   - Course close
