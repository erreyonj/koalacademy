# Module 1 music fieldwork — teacher key

Do not print this page for students. Print the grade packet double-sided. Students fill
**Before** in music class so the sheet is already started when they get on the bus.

This is a first-expedition pilot. The sheet asks one music question. The listening lessons
from the brainstorm (the loop, the Sudan podcast, Hendrix and Feliciano) stay in your room.
Modes and tie-dye stay off this round.

If a student can finish **After** without using a **During** note, that trip prompt did not
work. Use the pilot box after you read the stack.

## Grade 6 — *The Lightning Thief*

**EL questions.** What makes a myth? Why do myths matter?

**Music question.** What story is still being told here, and what did people have to do to
keep it alive?

**Site.** Classical Madison walk: a name, inscription, or myth on a building.

**After listening.** Robert Johnson, Prince performance stories, and “no pen” rappers
(Jay-Z and others). Then the prepared-loop activity: rap or sing along without writing.
Greek modes stay a later class. Nothing on the walk can teach them.

### Before script (about five minutes)

Say the music question once. Then:

> In *The Lightning Thief*, old Greek stories keep showing up in Percy’s world. Nobody
> invented those stories last week. People kept telling them, writing them, carving them,
> and performing them. On this walk you are not looking for a concert. You are looking for a
> story Madison is still telling in stone, metal, or letters — and for the work it took to
> leave that story standing.

Point to the three “watch for” bullets. Give the last minutes of Before for the four lines
under “On the trip I think I will notice…”

Hand the packets to the trip lead with the chaperone box facing up.

### After (next music class)

1. Students open the packet to **During**. They do not start the listening yet.
2. Tell the legend stories (Robert Johnson, Prince, no-pen rappers). Connect: a myth stays
   alive because somebody keeps performing it, not because it sits in a book.
3. Prepared loop: students try to rap or sing along without writing.
4. **After** writing: they must use one detail from the walk. If the page is blank, they
   write from memory of the stop, then note that the prompt came back empty.

## Grade 7 — *A Long Walk to Water*

**EL question.** Who are the Lost Children of Sudan, and what is their story?

**Music question.** What do people keep so a story is not lost, and whose history is in that
object?

**Site.** Whatever the school trip is (water, lake, or museum): one short prompt about what
the place is keeping on purpose. The real music evidence is the home task: the oldest
physical recording they can find without buying one.

**After listening.** Crosscurrents episode *Preserving the music of Sudan in Oakland*.
Killmonger / museum scene stays an optional aside if you have time. Do not put it on the
student sheet.

**Chaperone flex.** Do not collect these packets. Students still need the at-home block.

### Before script (about five minutes)

Say the music question once. Then:

> *A Long Walk to Water* is about people whose stories almost disappeared. Some stories live
> in a place — a plant, a lake, a museum label. Some only survive if somebody keeps an
> object: a tape, a record, a burned CD. On the trip, notice what that place is keeping on
> purpose. At home, find the oldest piece of physical music you can without going to a store.
> That object is the music evidence we will use next class.

Point to the three “watch for” bullets. Students fill “On the trip I think I will notice…”
Remind them the packet comes home with them.

### After (next music class)

1. Students put the object (or a photo / written description if they could not bring it) on
   the desk next to the packet.
2. Play / discuss the Crosscurrents episode. Physical media, and why conservation matters
   when a community has been displaced or silenced.
3. Optional aside, if it serves the room: Killmonger in the museum, a displaced person
   trying to get history back.
4. **After** writing: they must use the object, the site note, or both. A response that
   never names either did not use the packet.

## Grade 8 — *Summer of the Mariposas*

**EL questions.** Why do traditional stories show up in stories we tell now? How does a new
telling change them?

**Music question.** Whose version of this story is on display, and what would change if
someone else told it?

**Site.** A plaque, mural, monument, or public wording (capitol, monuments, Centro Hispano,
or local folklore sites).

**After listening.** Jimi Hendrix’s 1967 “Star-Spangled Banner” and José Feliciano’s 1968
rendition. Why those performances were controversial and reached mythic status. Tie-dye
stays off this pilot.

### Before script (about five minutes)

Say the music question once. Then:

> *Summer of the Mariposas* retells old folklore for new readers. McCall chose whose version
> you get. Public stories work the same way. A plaque, a mural, a monument is one telling.
> Somebody chose the words, the hero, and what got left off. On the trip, read the public
> story as a version — not as the only truth.

Point to the three “watch for” bullets. Students fill “On the trip I think I will notice…”

Hand the packets to the trip lead with the chaperone box facing up.

### After (next music class)

1. Students open **During** and star one line they will use.
2. Play Hendrix (1967), then Feliciano (1968). Same song, two tellings, two controversies.
3. **After** writing: they must use one detail from the trip (a name, a missing name, a
   loaded word on the plaque). Then they say what would change if the other musician’s
   version were the one on the wall.
