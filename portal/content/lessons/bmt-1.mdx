## The seven natural notes

Western music is built on seven natural notes: **A, B, C, D, E, F, and G**. Arrange them in a specific pattern of whole steps and half steps and you have a scale. That pattern is the shared vocabulary the rest of this course depends on — sample matching, drum design, and the original project at the end of the year all come back to it.

The pattern for a **major scale** is:

**whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half**

C major is the simplest case: C, D, E, F, G, A, B. Those are the white keys on a piano. A lot of songs live here because there are no sharps or flats to keep track of yet.

The pattern for a **natural minor scale** is:

**whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole**

A minor is C major's relative: A, B, C, D, E, F, G. Same notes, different starting place, different mood. Major tends to sound bright. Minor tends to sound heavier. You will hear that difference long before you can name the scale.

## Why C major first

Western music is often taught around C major because it is the simplest map. Learn the intervals of C major — the distances between those seven notes — and every other key is the same pattern shifted. The rest of Unit 1 (modes, relative keys, the staff) is that pattern seen from a new angle.

## What you should be able to do after this

1. Name the seven natural notes.
2. Sing or pick out the whole/half-step pattern of a major scale, starting on C.
3. Hear the difference between a major and a minor example and say which is which.
4. Point at a keyboard diagram and show where C major lives.

<Do title="Keyboard map">

On a keyboard diagram (or the classroom piano), find C. Walk the C major scale out loud: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Then start on A and walk A minor. Same notes — different home.

If a game or informal check shows someone cannot place those notes yet, that is a written corrective, not a new lecture. Paper follows doing.

</Do>
