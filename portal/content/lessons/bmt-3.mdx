## Two kinds of “related”

`BMT.1` already slipped in one relationship: **C major** and **A minor** share the same notes. `BMT.2` added new *boxes* — blues, Dorian, Phrygian. Today is about two cleaner pairs producers use every day:

- **Relative keys** — same key signature, different home note
- **Parallel keys** — same home note, different key signature

Same word (*related*) does not mean the same job. Mix them up and your samples clash, or your pitch shift lands in the wrong mood.

## Relative keys

**Relative keys** share a **key signature** — the same sharps or flats at the start of the staff — but they start on different notes.

**C major** and **A minor** are relative keys. Neither key signature writes any sharps or flats. The *notes on the shelf* are identical; the *door you walk through first* is different.

**Rule (major → relative minor):** from the major tonic, go down **three half steps** (a minor third). C → A.

**Rule (minor → relative major):** from the minor tonic, go up **three half steps**. A → C.

On guitar: play C major, then start the same shape on A — you are in the relative minor without retuning the world.

Bright major. Heavier minor. Same accidentals.

A minor — **relative** to C major. Same seven letters on the keyboard; home sits on A instead of C.

When you are digging for a second sample, relative keys are the fast friendship test: if the first loop is in C major, an A minor loop is often in the same note family without transposing anything.

## Parallel keys

**Parallel keys** share a **starting note** (the same *name tag*) but **not** the same key signature.

**C major** and **C minor** are parallel keys. Both start on **C**. C major has no sharps or flats. C natural minor has **E♭, A♭, and B♭** — three flats on the staff.

Relative = same signature, different home. Parallel = same home, different signature. Do not swap the definitions.

The emotional shift is the point. Parallel minor is not “sad major” as a joke — it is the **same letter** with a darker rule set. Producers use that on purpose: major hook, minor drop (or the reverse) without moving the root note the bass is glued to.

## Producer applications

Three jobs you will keep doing in Koala and beyond:

1. **Stack samples fast.** Relative keys (C major + A minor) often layer without a pitch war. Same key signature means the same “allowed notes” on the staff.
2. **Move material between keys.** Time-stretch and pitch tools nudge a loop up or down. Know whether you want a **relative** move (same mood family, new home) or a **parallel** flip (same home, new mood).
3. **Plan emotional turns.** A progression that visits the **relative minor** feels like walking into a room with the same furniture, different lighting. A **parallel** shift feels like the song put on a different coat while standing in the same spot.

You do not need to name the relationship before you like a sample. You name it so the **second** sample belongs in the same room — and so you know which knob to turn when it does not.

## What you should be able to do after this

1. Say the difference between **relative** and **parallel** keys in one sentence each.
2. Name the relative minor of **C major** and the relative major of **A minor**.
3. Name one thing **C major** and **C minor** share, and one thing they do not.
4. Hear **September** next to **Ain’t No Sunshine** and say which relationship you are hearing (relative keys).
