Write **your name** at the top of the page.

Music notation is a language. Languages are built on rules — and on how you break them.

Pick **one** language you know — English, or another if you speak it.

Write **2 or 3 rules** of that language. Short. Spelling or sound is enough. **Do not copy the examples.**

Examples (don't steal mine!):

- English: *"I" before "E" except after "C."*
- Spanish: *ll* makes a **Y** sound.

If you cannot think of any rules, write the **hardest words** you know how to spell.

Pens down when you are done.

  <Section name="The staff">
    <Slide copy kicker="The staff is the shared page">

A **staff** is five lines and four spaces. Pitch lives up and down those lines. Time lives left to right, cut into **measures** (bars) by vertical lines. If you can name the parts of that page, you can write an idea down so someone else — or you, next week — can play it back.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="The staff">

The two pictures below are frozen. They label the parts you need before anything else: **clef**, **time signature**, a **single note**, one **measure**, and the empty slot where a **key signature** would go.

  <Section name="Key signatures">
    <Slide copy kicker="Common key signatures">

A **key signature** is the cluster of sharps or flats at the start of the staff, right after the clef. It tells you which notes are raised or lowered for the whole piece, so you do not write an accidental on every note.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Common key signatures">

C major has none. That is why the second picture looks empty there — no space needed. G major takes one sharp (F♯). F major takes one flat (B♭). Minor keys can share a signature with a major key; A minor is the relative of C major, so it also looks blank.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Common key signatures">

We will put G, F, and a relative minor on the sandbox staff. Same slot as the purple oval.

</Slide>
  </Section>

  <Section name="4/4">
    <Slide copy kicker="4/4">

A **time signature** is two numbers stacked after the key signature. The top number is how many beats in a measure. The bottom number is what kind of note gets one beat. **4/4** means four beats, and the quarter note is one beat. Count **1-2-3-4**, then the bar line, then 1 again.

</Slide>

  <Section name="Rhythm">
    <Slide copy kicker="Rhythm is feeling and math at once">

**Rhythm** is how sound is placed in time. The math is the grid: beats, subdivisions, bars that add up. The feeling is where the hit actually sits — on the beat, ahead of it, behind it, or in the gaps.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Rhythm is feeling and math at once">

A quarter note fills one beat in 4/4. Two eighths split that beat. A rest is counted silence: the pulse keeps going even when nobody plays. Syncopation puts weight where the grid did not expect it. None of that replaces the count. The count is what you hold while the pattern leans.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Rhythm is feeling and math at once">

If you can keep 1-2-3-4 while the guitar tries to throw you, you own the grid. Then you can choose to sit on it like Latch, or drag it like Ocelot.

</Slide>
  </Section>

  <Section name="Keep the pulse">
    <Slide copy kicker="Keep the pulse">

1. *I do:* Mr. Erreyon counts 1-2-3-4 out loud, then plays a straight 4/4 figure on guitar so the room can lock in.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Keep the pulse">

2. *We do:* The room counts with him. Then the guitar starts missing on purpose — heavy syncopation, and some deliberate miscounting — to throw off student timekeeping.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Keep the pulse">

3. *You do:* Clap or count the quarter-note grid while the guitar leans. When you lose it, say so, find 1, and get back on.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Keep the pulse">

The sandbox can show the straight version of a rhythm after we feel it.

</Slide>
