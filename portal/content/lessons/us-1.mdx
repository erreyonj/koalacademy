## Unit 2 starts here

Unit 1 gave you theory and Koala navigation. Unit 2 asks a different question: **where did this practice come from**, and **what do you owe** the music you borrow?

**Sampling** means taking a piece of an existing recording and making it part of something new — a loop, a chop, a texture, a whole beat. Hip-hop did not invent the idea of reusing sound, but it turned it into a language. The iPad in your hand sits at the end of that line.

Your Do Now was not random. Sampling runs into **ownership**, **credit**, and **Integrity** fast. Hold that while you listen.

## Before the MPC: breaks and dub

In the 1970s Bronx, **DJ Kool Herc** (Clive Campbell) brought Jamaican sound-system culture to block parties. Instead of blending whole songs, he **looped the breaks** — the drum-heavy sections — and let the crowd dance longer. Two turntables, a mixer, the same break again and again.

That loop is an ancestor of what you do on a pad. No MPC yet. No Koala. Still: **old recording → new context**.

Jamaican **dub** producers in the 1960s and 70s were already treating the studio like an instrument — delays, echoes, stripping a mix to its bones and building something new. Herc’s breakbeat and dub’s remix culture meet in hip-hop’s DNA.

## The MPC and J Dilla

In **1988**, Akai shipped the **MPC** — **M**IDI **P**roduction **C**enter. Sixteen velocity-sensitive pads. Samples loaded in. Sequence in real time. Portable enough to leave the big studio and land in a bedroom or a tour bus.

**J Dilla** (James Yancey) is the producer this room keeps returning to: off-grid drums, low-end you feel in your chest, samples flipped until they barely resemble the source — on purpose. His **MPC 3000** lives in the National Museum of African American History and Culture. That is how central the machine became.

**Koala Sampler** on your iPad is basically a **mobile MPC**: pads, banks, sequence, chop, save. You already record onto pads in `KSN.1` and arrange in `KSN.2`. The lineage is not abstract — you are holding the next chapter.

Dilla famously left **quantize off** so drums could lean human. You met that idea in `KSN.2` and `KSN.4`. Same family of choices.

## Interpolation versus sampling

Two legal and musical words. Two different relationships to **ownership**.

| | **Sampling** | **Interpolation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you use | A slice of the **actual recording** | The **idea** re-performed in the studio |
| What you hear | The original timbre, room, player | New musicians playing something that **sounds like** the source |
| Credit / clearance | Often requires permission from the **master** owner | Often clears the **composition** (songwriter / publisher) |

Neither is “cheating.” Both can be art. Both can cross lines if you pretend you made what you did not — that is where **Integrity** returns.

**Jaco Pastorius** — *Portrait of Tracy* (1976). A bass standard: mostly **natural harmonics**, dreamy tone, almost no normal plucked notes.

Copyright and **fair use** are real and messy — this course will keep naming them. For today: if you use someone else’s work, **know which kind of use you are making** and **give credit where credit is owed**.

## Keys and frequency (why theory still matters)

`BMT.1`–`BMT.3` were not separate from sampling. They were prep.

- **Key:** a sample recorded in **A minor** fights a beat in **F major** unless you pitch it on purpose (`BMT.3`).
- **Frequency:** a boomy kick and a bright hi-hat live in different **ranges**. EQ and processing in Unit 2 only help if you know what you are carving (`BMT.5` drum names are the start).

Producers hear **timbre** — the color of the sound — as much as melody. A chopped snare is not just a note; it is a **band** of frequencies. That is why “I like this loop” is step one and “this loop **fits**” is step two.
