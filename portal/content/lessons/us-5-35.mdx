## A sound can travel

You have been learning about **sampling** — taking a piece of sound and using it to make something new. Today we ask a fun question: after someone makes a sound, **where does it go?**

Hold your Do Now. Some sounds are so good that people copy them again and again.

## "No Problem" — they made their own sound

**Chance the Rapper** made a song called *No Problem*. The big, happy **choir-and-horns** sound was built by two producers called **Brasstracks**.

Here is the cool part. It **sounds** like they grabbed an old record — but they didn't. They **recorded their own choir** a bunch of times to make it sound huge, then changed it and stacked lots of trumpets on top.

That is called **derivative sampling**: you **make your own sound first**, then reuse and shape it like a sample. Because they made it, the sound is **theirs**.

## When the sound leaves the song

Sometimes a **tiny piece** of a song — a few seconds, a word, a beat — pops out and travels on its own. People use that little sound in **videos they make themselves**, over and over.

Two things to notice:

- What travels is usually the **sound**, not the whole song.
- People do not always know **who made it first**.

That is the same idea from our other lessons: if you use someone's sound, it is kind and fair to **know where it came from** and **say so**.

## The same idea in podcasts

You do not have to make beats to use this. **Podcasts** — shows people listen to — reuse sound too: little music beds under a story, sound effects, and clips. Being a good **listener and storyteller** is its own kind of sampling.

## What you should be able to do after this

1. Say what **derivative sampling** means — make your own sound, then reuse it.
2. Explain how *No Problem* sounds sampled but is **made from scratch**.
3. Tell what usually **travels** — a small sound, not the whole song.
4. Give one place sounds get reused **besides music** (a podcast or a video).
