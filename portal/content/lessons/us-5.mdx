  <Section name="No Problem">
    <Slide copy kicker="No Problem">

**Chance the Rapper** — *No Problem* (feat. Lil Wayne & 2 Chainz), from *Coloring Book* (2016). Produced by **Brasstracks** — Ivan Jackson and Conor Rayne. It won a Grammy for Best Rap Performance.

</Slide>
    <Slide kicker="Listen">
      <YouTubeEmbed
        id="mso-2qFm1Oo"
        title="No Problem (Official Video)"
        artist="Chance the Rapper ft. 2 Chainz & Lil Wayne"
      />
    </Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="No Problem">

Here is the twist. That triumphant gospel-choir sound reads like a classic **old-Kanye chop** — like they lifted a record. They didn't. **There are no samples in it.** Brasstracks built the source themselves: they recorded a choir four times over so it sounded like sixteen singers, then **pitched those takes up and down an octave**, and layered roughly a **hundred trumpets** on top.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="No Problem">

That is **derivative sampling**: instead of borrowing a recording, you **make your own source material** and then treat it like a sample — chop it, pitch it, layer it, arrange it. Same production muscle as `US.1`–`US.4`, pointed at audio you own from the first second.

</Slide>
  </Section>

  <Section name="Make your own source">
    <Slide kicker="Listen">
      <YouTubeEmbed
        id="NW3QVXxHUVM"

        artist="Brasstracks · Genius Deconstructed"

    <Slide copy kicker="Make your own source">

Why work this hard to fake a sample instead of clearing a real one?

- **Ownership is clean.** No master to license, no publisher to chase (`US.4`). The sound is theirs.
- **The sound is a signature.** Brasstracks call their thing "future brass." That identity is the point — and it is what got them the placement. People kept saying their beats sounded like Chance should be on them; they tweeted exactly that, Chance retweeted and followed, they DM'd him. **Staying true to a voice opened the door.**

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Make your own source">

That is the through-line into `DYS.1` later: a recognizable personal sound is not decoration, it is leverage.

</Slide>
  </Section>

  <Section name="When the sound leaves">
    <Slide copy kicker="When the sound leaves the track">

A sample does not always stay inside a song. Sometimes a **two-second gesture** — a vocal ad-lib, a chopped phrase, a drum fill — breaks off and becomes a **format**: the "sound" thousands of people reuse across videos they make themselves.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="When the sound leaves the track">

Notice what actually travels. It is usually **the sound, not the whole song**. And once it spreads, two things come apart:

- **Origin** — who made it first.
- **Reach** — how far it went.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="When the sound leaves the track">

A sound can be everywhere and still be **uncredited or misattributed**. That is the exact `US.4` ownership question, moved from the studio to the feed. **Borrowing with purpose** still applies when the borrower is a whole audience, not one producer.

</Slide>
  </Section>

  <Section name="Same muscle">
    <Slide copy kicker="Same muscle, different room">

You do not have to make beats to use any of this. **Podcasting** runs on the same move — take existing audio and **place it with intention**: theme beds, stingers, drops, and clip-based storytelling. A sharp **listener, curator, or editor** is doing sampling work without ever opening Koala.

</Slide>
    <Slide copy kicker="Same muscle, different room">

The ethics come along for the ride: **attribution**, **licensed vs. lifted** audio, and knowing **which kind of use** you are making. That is the bridge into Unit 3's rights work (`SRP`).

</Slide>
