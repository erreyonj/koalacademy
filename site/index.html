01 · The year in one breath

A K–8 music program built on doing.

Kindergarten through fifth grade is unplugged-first — games, movement, singing, and intentional listening, organized around seven concept strands. Sixth through eighth grade runs the Koalacademy production curriculum: music theory in service of making, sampling history, drum design, song form, and sampling rights, building toward an original project sampled from songs the students choose and performed live.

The through-line is listening. Every grade carries a small set of Vanguard Songs all year and keeps coming back to them as skills grow — so the same recording sounds different in May than it did in October.

02 · Two bands, one trajectory

K–5 builds the ear. 6–8 puts it to work.

K–5 · Unplugged first

Unplugged-first — screens and seat work stay rare. Young musicians keep the beat with their bodies, hunt the room for sounds, sing back melodies, and learn that music has parts that repeat and change. Everything — every game, lesson, and procedure — is taught I do / We do / You do, with the teacher's guitar leading demonstrations.

Content is organized by seven strands, each feeding directly into the production work waiting in middle school:

  • BEATsteady beat, meter, rhythm, rests
  • PITCHhigh/low, melody, major vs. minor mood
  • TIMBREsound sources and how to describe them
  • FORMrepetition, contrast, song shapes
  • NOTATEsymbols, the staff, reading rhythm
  • LISTENintentional listening and response
  • CREATEcomposing, improvising, sharing

6–8 · Koalacademy production

Middle school enters a five-unit production course taught on Koala Sampler: theory and navigation, sampling and drum design, song forms and sampling rights, then defining your own sound — and a final project sampled from three songs each student chooses, justifies, and honors before performing the result live.

Theory isn't front-loaded; it's spiraled across the year and introduced when the music being made needs it. Device-dependent work clusters onto reserved device days, so no 1:1 access is assumed.

  • Unit 1 Music theory + Koala navigation
  • Unit 2 Sampling + digital drum design
  • Unit 3 Song forms + sampling rights
  • Unit 4 Defining your sound + artist appreciation
  • Unit 5 Advanced effects + live performance

03 · Grade by grade

Nine grades, nine snapshots.

Tap a grade to see what the year looks like from that seat.

KMusic through play

Kindergartners keep a steady beat with their bodies, freeze on silences, hunt for sounds around the room, echo short songs, and learn that music has parts that repeat and change. Everything is movement, voice, and games.

1The beat becomes theirs

First graders keep the beat without the teacher counting, echo short rhythms, describe sounds with a growing timbre vocabulary, and start hearing A/B section changes without cues.

2Internalizing the pulse

Silent Ball arrives — passes land on the downbeat only. Rhythms stretch to two bars with multiple rests, and the first real notation reading begins: matching heard rhythms to simple written ones.

3Playing with knowledge

Third graders play with what they know, not just how they move: trivia games enter as review, listening responses become full sentences, and reading simple 4/4 rhythms becomes expected rather than introduced.

4Vocabulary as material

Musical Taboo forces students to describe concepts by their properties. Note names land on the staff, Silent Ball moves to off-beats, and exit tickets become a routine written artifact.

5The bridge year

Fifth grade consolidates everything the strands built — internal 4/4 counting, subdivision, staff reading, full-song form, timbre vocabulary — so sixth grade can walk into production without a theory front-load. First exposure to what sampling is happens here, by ear, without devices.

6Enter production

Sixth grade enters the Koalacademy curriculum: theory in service of making, sampling history, drum design, song form, sampling rights, and an original project sampled from songs the students choose. The listening lens: intentional listening — emotion and basic structure.

7Structure and identity

Seventh grade sharpens the lens toward deeper structural awareness, contrast, and stylistic identity. Extended theory — modes, relative and parallel keys — lands here, tied to real sample-matching work.

8Interpretive discourse

Eighth grade runs the course closest to its original form, carrying the full final-project arc with the least scaffolding. The lens: restraint vs. complexity, artistic intent, and meaning — by spring, discussion approaches high-school level.

04 · Vanguard Songs

The same songs, all year, on purpose.

Every grade is assigned a small set of anchor recordings that stay constant from September to June. They work the way a novel works in an English class: a fixed text that students return to as their vocabulary, skill, and ear grow. Producers study a small canon deeply rather than skimming hundreds of records — familiarity is the point.

"What do you hear now that you didn't hear before?"

— asked in the fall, and again in the spring
  • K–2 One anchor per grade — steady beat, sound identification, feelings words, echo singing. Three-to-five-minute sittings; bodies and single words are the response format.
  • 3–5 One to two anchors — section signals, instrument naming, first "why" questions, and short journal lines with drawing and sentence frames.
  • 6–8 Three anchors per grade and a running listening journal. Every concept — form, rhythm, harmony, production, sampling — eventually points back at these three records.

Song lists lock before the year starts and don't change mid-year. Selections favor a clear beat, strong section contrast, distinguishable timbres, and lyrics that hold up in a classroom.

05 · How class feels

If you walked in mid-game.

Most days you'd catch a room mid-game. Every game targets a strand, and watching students play is the assessment.

  • BEAT

    Beat Freeze

    Students walk a circle stepping on the beat while a rhythm loops — and freeze on every rest. Who freezes late is following neighbors instead of hearing the silence.

  • BEAT

    Silent Ball

    The ball may only be passed on the downbeat. No talking, all counting — internal pulse made visible.

  • TIMBRE

    Timbre Scavenger

    Teams hunt the room for objects that make a target kind of sound — smooth, scratchy, ringing, buzzy — then defend their find to the class.

  • FORM

    Form Detective

    Students signal the moment a song changes sections — played directly on the grade's Vanguard Song, so the anchor record and the form work reinforce each other.

Standing decisions behind every lesson

  • I do / We do / You do. Every game, lesson, and procedure is demonstrated, guided, then handed over.
  • Guitar-first. The teacher's guitar leads demonstrations; the classroom piano serves as the visual aid.
  • Theory is spiraled, not front-loaded. Concepts arrive when a lesson or project needs them, and classes accelerate on demonstrated prior knowledge.
  • Written work leans corrective. From the middle grades up, exit tickets, journals, and fill-ins also grade and track progress — the doing is still the spine of the course.
  • Devices are scheduled, not assumed. K–5 stays unplugged; 6–8 clusters production work onto reserved device days.

06 · What students walk away with

Growth you can hear.

  • K–2

    Keeps a steady beat in a group, freezes on a rest, names what a sound is like, sings back what they hear, and can say how a piece of music feels — with the music as their evidence.

  • 3–5

    Reads and claps simple rhythms, predicts a song's next section, describes sound in real vocabulary, keeps short listening journals, and composes and notates first patterns of their own.

  • 6–8

    Builds a complete original project from three self-chosen sampled sources, explains the theory and the rights behind every choice, defends a developing sound of their own — and performs the work live.
