Vanguard Songs · Grades 3–5

One Companion. A weekly lens.

Third through fifth grade share one Companion — the same track grade 7 uses as Primary. Students get the student guide, sentence frames, and journal lines about a record they already know by heart.

Status: locked Companion. 3–5 carry “Chicago” — Michael Jackson (grade 7 Primary / [3-5 Companion]). Depth stays lighter than middle school: strand lenses and journal lines, not full discourse threads.

This is the band where listening becomes an artifact. Third graders write sentences; fourth and fifth point a different lens at the same record each week — form, drums, timbre — and fifth grade pre-loads middle school with drum roles by ear.

3

“Chicago” — Michael Jackson

Grade 3 · Companion (grade 7 Primary)

Third grade's Companion carries the first “why” questions: major or minor, and what in the music tells you so.

  • Fall. Predict the next section out loud before it arrives.
  • Winter. Major vs. minor mood by ear, with the recording as evidence.
  • Spring. Journal line with a sentence frame: “Today I noticed ______ in the music.”
4

“Chicago” — Michael Jackson

Grade 4 · Companion (grade 7 Primary)

Fourth grade names verse and chorus on this record, then describes the production sounds it uses — the vocabulary work Musical Taboo drills all year.

  • Fall. Map the full song form: verse, chorus, and what marks the boundary.
  • Winter. Instrument families by ear; describe two recorded sounds by their properties.
  • Spring. Short journal entry against that week's lens.
5

“Chicago” — Michael Jackson

Grade 5 · Companion (grade 7 Primary) · bridge-year reference

The bridge-year Companion should reward drum listening: kick, snare, and hat roles need to be findable by ear, because sixth grade rebuilds them from scratch.

  • Fall. Subdivision felt and named against the recording.
  • Winter. Drum roles by ear — kick, snare, hat — ahead of digital drum design.
  • Spring. Journal entries that answer “why,” not just “what.”

The question that bookends the year

“What do you hear now that you didn't hear before?”

— asked in the fall, and again in the spring
